# Offer design frameworks

Brite-adapted reference for evaluating and designing outbound offers. Vertical-agnostic. Consumed by vertical playbooks and preset-composition issues to separate structurally-sound offers from consultant-speak and gimmicky guarantees.

These frameworks answer two questions: *is this offer worth opening an email for?* (frontend design) and *is the guarantee credible enough that a buyer would risk signing?* (backend design). Skip either lens and you end up with polished copy selling a broken offer.

---

## Hormozi value equation

The core Hormozi claim: perceived value is a ratio with two levers on top and two on the bottom. You move any of the four and the offer becomes stronger or weaker — mechanically.

```
Value = (Dream Outcome × Perceived Likelihood) / (Time Delay × Effort + Sacrifice)
```

- **Dream Outcome** — the end-state the buyer actually wants, stated in their own language. Bigger, more specific, more emotionally resonant dream = higher numerator. "More reservations for your holiday season" is weaker than "Sold-out weekends for Zoo Lights without discounting tickets."
- **Perceived Likelihood** — how believable it is that *your* offer delivers the dream for *this specific buyer*. Case studies, named customers, underwriting via guarantee all push this up. Generic claims push it down.
- **Time Delay** — the gap between paying and receiving value. A 6-month installation before the event pays back is high delay. A paid diagnostic that produces a plan in 2 weeks is low delay.
- **Effort + Sacrifice** — everything the buyer has to spend or give up beyond dollars: political capital, procurement cycles, "things I need to figure out myself," risk of looking dumb in front of the board. Low-effort offers (vendor handles end-to-end) score higher.

**The four Hormozi guarantee types:**

| Type | Definition | B2B-outbound example |
|------|------------|----------------------|
| Conditional | Refund or re-performance if a defined criterion fails | "If first-season lighting revenue < production cost, we refund the production fee" |
| Unconditional | Refund no questions asked within a window | "30-day no-questions refund on the paid audit" |
| Anti-guarantee | Explicit disclaimer of what you won't promise — sharpens credibility on what you *will* promise | "We won't guarantee ticket sales — we will guarantee installation meets the event date or we eat the overtime" |
| Implied (performance / rev-share) | Vendor compensation tied to the outcome the buyer actually cares about | "Flat production fee + rev-share on incremental ticket revenue over prior-year benchmark" |

**Worked example — Offer E production-finance applied to a generic zoo:**

A zoo director wants a holiday lights event (Dream Outcome: a sold-out, press-worthy holiday season). Offer A ("pay for production upfront, we install, you keep ticket revenue") has *high Time Delay* (pay in Q2, revenue lands in Q4) and *high Effort + Sacrifice* (director has to get the full capex through a budget committee before the event exists). Perceived Likelihood is average — another vendor pitched the same thing last year.

Offer E flips Time Delay and Effort. Brite finances production against Year 1 ticket revenue. The director pays nothing upfront (Time Delay → near-zero), doesn't defend a capex line item (Effort + Sacrifice → much lower), and the financing itself is a credibility signal (Perceived Likelihood ↑ — Brite only does this when they believe in the numerator). Same Dream Outcome, but the denominator dropped by an order of magnitude. The offer converts at a dramatically different rate, even though the event itself is identical.

## Brunson value ladder

Three tiers of offer, ascending in price and commitment. The principle that breaks most outbound programs: **the frontend's job is qualifying and acquiring, not making money on its own.** Treat frontend as a standalone revenue line and you'll under-invest in it, which kills your acquisition funnel.

- **Frontend** — low-commitment bait. For B2B cold outbound, the frontend is almost always the **email CTA**: a custom audit, a benchmark report, a 30-minute diagnostic call, a site survey. It should be cheap or free for the buyer, valuable standalone, and effortful for the vendor to deliver. Its output points at the backend.
- **Mid-tier** — the in-between step that exists in some sales motions and not others. A paid workshop, a deep-dive diagnostic, a pilot project. Not every outbound motion has a mid-tier; many jump from frontend straight to backend.
- **Backend** — the high-ticket, done-for-you engagement. In Brite's world, this is the full holiday lights engagement, the permanent architectural-lighting install, the multi-year Brite Supply contract. This is where the margin lives.

**B2B-outbound specifics.** In cold email, the entire job of the email is frontend conversion — get the prospect to accept the free/low-cost diagnostic. Nothing else. Stacking backend commitments into the first email ("sign up for our $250K holiday program") is the most common frontend mistake: you ask for backend commitment with only a cold-email's worth of trust. The ladder exists specifically so backend commitment gets sold *after* the frontend has built trust and surfaced fit signals.

## Abraham risk-reversal

**Principle:** the more risk you take off the prospect, the higher the close rate. Every hesitation a buyer has is some form of "what if this doesn't work and I'm the one holding the bag?" — risk-reversal exists to move that weight from buyer to vendor.

Abraham and Hormozi sit at different layers. **Abraham is philosophical** — it frames the strategic move (who should carry risk in this deal, and why). **Hormozi is structural** — it chooses the mechanism (which guarantee type, with what trigger and what remedy). A good offer usually starts with an Abraham-style decision (we will underwrite the risk the buyer fears most) and then picks a Hormozi guarantee type to instantiate it. Inverting the order — picking a guarantee mechanism first, then working out what risk it reverses — usually produces gimmicks.

## Frontend-offer characteristics (B2B outbound)

A frontend offer that clears these six checks is usually worth testing. Miss two or more and the email doesn't convert, regardless of copy quality.

1. **Named + specific deliverable.** The buyer can picture the artifact before agreeing. *Specific:* "First-year lighting plan for [Venue Name] — layout diagram, wattage budget, run-time schedule, and ticket-revenue model." *Generic:* "We can help with your lighting."
2. **Standalone value.** The deliverable is useful even if the prospect never buys the backend. *Specific:* an audit report the director can take to their board regardless of whether they hire Brite. *Generic:* a qualifying "discovery call" that's transparently a sales pitch with no artifact.
3. **Low friction.** Single stakeholder, no procurement cycle, no NDA dance to get started. *Specific:* "30-minute call with you + one walk-around of the site." *Generic:* multi-stakeholder, legal review, steering committee sign-off before delivery begins.
4. **Qualifying.** The delivery itself filters for budget, timeline, and decision authority — so the vendor learns whether to invest in a backend pitch. *Specific:* the audit requires the prospect to share last season's revenue + attendance; refusal is itself a signal. *Generic:* a brochure that anyone can accept, signaling nothing.
5. **Natural segue to backend.** The diagnostic conclusion points at the full engagement. *Specific:* audit ends with a scoped statement of work + price for the full install, ready to sign. *Generic:* audit ends with "let us know if you're interested."
6. **Effortful-for-vendor (commitment bias).** The prospect perceives value because the vendor obviously sank real work into the deliverable — 2-3 days of analysis, not a 10-minute template. *Specific:* site-specific numbers, site-specific recommendations, prepared in a named document. *Generic:* a PDF that looks identical to every other prospect's PDF.

## Backend-guarantee characteristics

A backend guarantee that clears these four checks is credible enough to shift risk perception. Miss any and the guarantee becomes either un-underwriteable or gimmicky (or both).

1. **Quantified "doesn't work" definition.** The failure condition is a number, not a feeling.
   - *Good:* "First-season ticket revenue from the holiday event < production cost" — a single dollar figure, measurable, observable by both parties.
   - *Bad:* "If you're not satisfied with the results" — subjective, always disputable, effectively un-guaranteeable.
2. **Metric aligned with buyer's actual success criteria (not vanity).** The guarantee metric is the thing the buyer's P&L, board, or boss actually cares about.
   - *Good:* "Reservation lift vs. same-season prior year" for a zoo holiday event — directly ties to revenue, which is what the director reports on.
   - *Bad:* "Photo-share benchmark on social" — vanity metric; it isn't what the director is measured on, so the guarantee doesn't reduce the *real* risk the buyer is carrying.
3. **Reasonable for vendor to underwrite.** The downside is bounded and the vendor has actual control over the metric.
   - *Good:* "First-season-breakeven-or-no-pay" — downside capped at production cost, and Brite controls the production work that drives the outcome.
   - *Bad:* "We'll refund 10x your spend" — unbounded, not credible, not underwriteable; prospects read this as marketing bluster, not commitment.
4. **Real urgency via performance alignment (not countdown-timer scarcity).** The reason to act now is that vendor incentives are already aligned with buyer outcomes.
   - *Good:* "We get paid when the event lights up, so we want it ready on time as badly as you do" — structural alignment; urgency comes from the incentive system, not the sales page.
   - *Bad:* "Sign this week for 15% off" — manufactured urgency; tells the buyer the price was negotiable all along, which *reduces* perceived value.

## How to use this reference

**For vertical playbooks (Phase 2 / R-4..R-9 — zoos, aquariums, casinos, hotels-resorts, ski-resorts, sports-stadiums).** When proposing candidate offers for a vertical, run each candidate through the Frontend-offer checklist and each candidate backend guarantee through the Backend-guarantee checklist. Offers that miss two or more frontend checks, or any backend check, get rejected before they make it into the playbook. Document rejected offers briefly so the next session doesn't re-propose them.

**For preset composition (Phase 4 / R-10..R-15 — zoos, aquariums, casinos, hotels-resorts, ski-resorts, sports-stadiums presets).** When composing `email-copywriting/presets/<vertical>/*.md` files, name the Value Equation lever the offer leans on most heavily — "Dream Outcome dominant" for an aspirational signature-event offer, "Time Delay dominant" for a production-financing offer, "Effort + Sacrifice dominant" for a turnkey done-for-you offer. This single tag keeps email copy honest: the subject line and opening hook should pull on the same lever the offer pulls on.

**Anti-pattern flag.** If a proposed offer can't name a specific Hormozi guarantee type *and* fails the Backend-guarantee checklist, it's consultant-speak — send it back. The two failure modes are correlated: offers without structural guarantees tend to default to vanity-metric promises and countdown-timer urgency, because those are the only handles available when the underlying offer isn't underwriteable.
